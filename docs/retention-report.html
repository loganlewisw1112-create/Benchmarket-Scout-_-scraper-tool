The retention report — one problem, and the plan to kill it

A great report is the easiest thing in the world to use once and forget.

Benchmark Scout won the first click and lost the second. 1,000 simulated field reports say it a dozen ways: the report dazzles once, then dies. This brief fuses that evidence with the plan to force a second visit — the product loop, the price, and the exact people to sell it to — pressure-tested against the operators who already solved retention. One question decides everything: do accounts come back next month.

The one-line read

The first report is excellent. There is no second one — no monitoring, no alert, no account — so the tool reads as one-and-done, and that single gap is the entire −21.6. Do not add features to the report. Turn the report into a monitored, alerting, account-based subscription, then aim every ounce of acquisition at the one audience that needs it monthly. The money is already on the table; you just haven't given anyone a reason to come back and spend it.

§01

The one problem worth solving

A personalized competitor report is the best cold-open you can send and the fastest thing on earth to abandon. Stop reading the symptoms as separate problems. The negative NPS, the “useful once” quotes, the flat sentiment — one disease, one diagnosis: acquisition works, the bucket leaks, and you are paying to fill a bucket that empties itself.

The product is not broken — it turns a stranger into “huh, that's actually useful” in thirty seconds flat. It is missing exactly one thing: a second visit. So the fix is not a longer roadmap; it is a single, non-negotiable move — install a reason to return before you spend another dollar on acquisition. Retention is not one metric among many here. It is the entire business, and it is the lens every section below is written through. Everything that does not create a second visit is a distraction until this is solved.

From the shelfThe Lean Startup · Eric Ries

Ries separates the “sticky” engine of growth — where you win by keeping users faster than you lose them — from the vanity of raw signups. A tool with no return visit runs the paid engine on a leaky tank. The only number that proves an engine is turning is cohort retention: does the group who ran a report in Week 0 run another in Week 4. That is our north star; every feature and email below is judged against it.

§02

Who ran it — and who to actually chase

Overwhelmingly local home & professional-service SMBs — the exact niche the real-only OSM/website pipeline serves. Owners run it themselves, and here is the trap: the owner needs this report twice a year. The agency rebuilds it every month, by hand, for every client. Sell to the owner and you are farming churn. Sell to the agency and you are selling a chore they'd kill to delete. That gap is the whole go-to-market.

§06

The retention loop — the whole ballgame

You do not fix a one-and-done tool with a better report. You fix it with a habit. Nir Eyal's Hook Model is the precise shape of that habit — four stages — and Benchmark Scout can build every one of them from features users already begged for. This section is the company.

01 · Trigger

The email that pulls them back

A monitored change fires an external trigger — no willpower required from the user.

→ change alerts + monthly digest
02 · Action

One click to the report

Open the saved report to see what moved. The simplest possible action, already built.

→ saved business + market
03 · Variable reward

Something changed

A rival's rank slipped, a new competitor opened, a review score shifted — never the same twice.

→ score / rank history, trends
04 · Investment

They make it theirs

Add a competitor to watch, set alert thresholds, fix a flagged gap. Each act deepens the next loop.

→ watchlist + fix-first playbook
From the shelfHooked · Nir Eyal

The loop above is the product's missing second act. Today's one-time report has a trigger and an action but zero variable reward and zero investment — two of the four stages are simply absent, so the habit can't form and the NPS can't recover. Half a loop is no loop. Ship all four stages together and the tool stops being something you use and becomes something you check — the exact release that erases the −21.6. Ship them apart and you've shipped nothing.

Build in this order

Sequenced so the first paid release fixes NPS and mints the flagship tier in one shot. Nothing here is speculative — every phase closes a gap the corpus named out loud. Do not reorder it.

P·0

Activation fixes pre-monetization

(1) URL confirm / retry / manual override so the 35% of unreadable own-sites can self-correct at input. (2) Widen-radius fallback + “add a competitor manually” for the 10.5% thin-market runs. (3) A single prioritized “fix this first” line on the free report — resolves “not sure what to do” and teases Recon.

Fixes: 35% incomplete self-audit · 10.5% thin markets · neutral-47% “what do I do first”
P·1

Close the loop the whole ballgame

Accounts + saved business/market, recurring monitoring (monthly default, weekly option), change alerts + monthly digest, score/rank history with trend charts, a competitor watchlist, and an AI plain-English summary + prioritized playbook. This is the release that turns a tool into a subscription. If you build one thing this quarter, it is this — everything else is a rounding error beside it.

Ships the Recon (Pro) tier · addresses 69% recurring-update demand + 4 of the top-5 requests
P·2

Deepen the signal credibility

Bring in reviews/ratings and Google Business Profile / maps-pack visibility — the #1 request and the 39% “ratings N/A” gap — plus a verified-facts layer. A policy decision is required (see §10): do this via owner-authorized first-party connections to stay real-only, not scraped third-party data.

Ships toward Command (Business) · #1 requested feature (14.7%)
P·3

Accounts & scale expansion revenue

Multi-location / multiple markets under one account, team seats, CSV / API export, benchmark-vs-national-industry, saved watchlists. Unlocks the franchise/multi-location segment (6% of base, high retention) and net-revenue expansion inside existing accounts.

Completes Command · targets the 8.4% multi-location + 6% franchise demand
P·4

Agency & white-label highest ARPU

White-label / branded reports, a client dashboard across many businesses, bulk runs, priority processing. 11% of the base are agencies and roughly half explicitly want white-label — the smallest segment but the highest revenue per account, and the beachhead §02 tells us to lead with.

Ships the Ops (Agency) tier · ~111 agency accounts, median $29/client

§08

Go-to-market — reach the beachhead

You're hunting one animal: the small shop that already does competitor research for local clients on a schedule. Budget, recurring need, a roster that multiplies every seat. Qualify ruthlessly before you spend a single email — a great pitch to the wrong list is still a wasted send.

Go after them if

  • 1–20-person local SEO / digital marketing shop, or a solo consultant
  • Their clients are physical businesses — dentists, restaurants, contractors, clinics
  • They send clients monthly reports (their site will say so)
  • They already pay for BrightLocal, Semrush, or a rank tracker
  • They white-label their deliverables

Skip them if

  • National brand or enterprise agency with its own analysts
  • They build their own data pipelines
  • E-commerce or SaaS clients only — no local angle
  • One-person shop with one or two clients — won't stick
Where they are Search “local SEO agency [city]” and “[city] digital marketing.” Comb the local-SEO categories on Clutch and UpCity. Watch who posts rank-tracker screenshots on LinkedIn. Sit in the marketing Slack groups, r/SEO, and r/agency — the people complaining about client reporting are the whole list.

Lead with the report, not the pitch

Run their report before you write a word. The email is just the envelope — the report is the pitch, already finished, already useful. Be specific, then get out of the way. The highlighted fields swap per prospect.

From the shelfNever Split the Difference · Voss  /  The Mom Test · Fitzpatrick

Voss proved that inviting a “no” disarms people faster than any pitch — “say so and I'll leave it” pulls more replies than one more sell, which is why the nudge and the breakup carry the whole sequence. And Fitzpatrick's rule runs through the opener: lead with a specific thing you already did, never a hypothetical. The report is the ask. There is nothing to over-promise, because the value is already sitting in their inbox.

§09

What “working” looks like — and in what order

Three numbers decide whether this is a business — and they only count in order. Nail one before you chase the next. And do not scale a funnel you cannot see: without analytics, every number below is a feeling.

3–5%

Cold-email reply rate. Below 3% means the list or the copy is wrong, not the product. Owners reply more than execs — use it.

< 1 sitting

Time to first finished report on a business they care about. Miss this and they don't come back — it's the activation gate.

Week 4

Do accounts return and run another report a month later? The north star from §01 — the only number that says you have a business.

Order of operations

  1. Turn on analytics. Signup, report run, share, return. Everything below is guesswork without it — and it's the one gap in the product today.
  2. Hand-run 25–50 reports. Send them yourself, get 5–10 agencies on a call. Learn what they'd actually pay for before automating anything.
  3. Add billing and the agency tier once someone says the words “I'd pay for this.” Convert the warmest waitlist names first.
  4. Build scheduled monthly reports and change alerts. New competitor opened, a rival changed pricing or started hiring — the reason they stay, so the feature that matters most.
  5. Let shares and SEO pages compound in the background while outreach carries the near term.
From the shelfThe Lean Startup · Ries  /  The Mom Test · Fitzpatrick

Step 1 is Ries's entire thesis in one line: signups are a vanity metric, cohort return is an actionable one, and you cannot improve a retention number you refuse to measure — so it comes before everything. Steps 2–3 are the Mom Test with its sleeves rolled up: hand-run the reports and get on the calls before you write a line of billing code. Let behavior shape the agency tier. A survey's optimism has already lied to you once — see the 80%.

§10

The one real decision to make

The top data-depth requests — reviews/ratings, Google Business Profile, verified facts — smash straight into Benchmark Scout's founding vow: real-only, free sources, no paid APIs, nothing behind a login. You cannot hand-wave this one. Pick a side, on purpose, and know exactly what it costs.

Option A — Stay strictly real-only

Unlock reviews/GBP through owner-authorized first-party connections — the official, free Google Business Profile API the owner opts into for their own listing. Preserves provenance integrity and the “didn't feel made-up” trust that's already the top praise.

Recommended. Keeps the brand promise; still ships the #1 request.

Option B — Paid data on paid tiers

Introduce licensed third-party review/ratings data on Command+, clearly labelled by provenance and walled off from the free real-only report. Faster and broader coverage, but dilutes the founding stance and adds COGS per report.

Viable as a later add-on — not the opening move.

From the shelfObviously Awesome · April Dunford

Positioning is won by owning one word, and Benchmark Scout already owns “real” — the top promoter praise is, verbatim, “didn't feel made-up.” Option B sells that word for coverage. Option A keeps it and still ships the #1 feature. The schema already carries a provenance.policy: "real-only" flag, so owner-authorized first-party sources drop in without cracking the one thing users trust. This isn't a close call. Lead with A, and don't relitigate it.
